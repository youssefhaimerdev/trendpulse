Presidential Records Act Ruling 2026: Federal Judge Orders White House to Comply After DOJ Called Law Unconstitutional

U.S. District Judge John Bates issued an emergency order on May 20 requiring White House staff, the NSC, and DOGE to comply with the Presidential Records Act — directly overruling a DOJ opinion that the 1978 law was unconstitutional. Here is the full breakdown.

By Daily Pulse Staff·May 21, 2026·4 min read·👁 33,107
Share:

A federal judge on Wednesday ordered the White House to comply with the Presidential Records Act, directly blocking a Department of Justice opinion that had declared the 1978 law unconstitutional. U.S. District Judge John Bates of the D.C. District Court issued the preliminary injunction after finding the White House was at "substantial risk" of destroying official documents that legally belong to the American public. The order takes effect May 26, 2026.

Presidential Records Act Ruling — Key Facts
  • Judge: John D. Bates, U.S. District Court for D.C.
  • Order type: Preliminary injunction (not a final ruling)
  • Takes effect: May 26, 2026 at 9:00 AM
  • Who is covered: White House Office, National Security Council, US DOGE Service, all presidential advisors
  • Who is NOT covered: President Trump, VP Vance, DOJ, National Archives
  • Compliance deadline: White House must file notice of steps taken by May 28
  • Brought by: American Historical Association, American Oversight, Citizens for Ethics (CREW)

What triggered the ruling?

In April 2026, the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel issued a legal opinion declaring the Presidential Records Act "unconstitutional" and stating that President Trump "need not further comply" with the law. The DOJ argued presidential papers are personal property — the same position the White House took before Congress passed the PRA in 1978, in the wake of the Watergate scandal.

Three organizations — the American Historical Association, American Oversight, and Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington (CREW) — sued immediately, arguing the administration's position threatened to allow the wholesale destruction of government documents that federal law designates as public property. Judge Bates agreed with the plaintiffs and issued the emergency order within weeks of the lawsuit being filed.

What exactly does the order require?

The injunction requires covered personnel to: preserve all presidential and vice presidential records as defined by the act; refrain from using ephemeral or personal messaging accounts — specifically including Signal — to conduct official business without simultaneously preserving copies on official government systems; and maintain records retention policies that fully comply with the PRA. The White House must also circulate a copy of the court order to all covered employees and file a compliance report with the court by May 28.

The Signal provision is particularly significant. Reports earlier this year revealed senior officials including National Security Advisor Mike Waltz had used Signal — an encrypted app that auto-deletes messages — to discuss official government business. The DOJ's opinion would have provided legal cover for that practice. The court's order eliminates it.

"The court recognized the serious danger posed by the administration's attempt to cast aside longstanding federal law. Presidential records belong to the American people." — Chioma Chukwu, Executive Director, American Oversight

Why doesn't the order cover President Trump directly?

Judge Bates was careful to note that courts generally cannot directly order the president to perform official duties — a longstanding constitutional principle called separation of powers. The order therefore targets the staff and advisory bodies who generate, handle, and preserve records, rather than Trump or Vice President Vance personally. In practice, this distinction is largely procedural: if White House staff complies, the records will be preserved regardless of whether the president is personally named in the order.

What is the Presidential Records Act?

The Presidential Records Act was signed into law by President Jimmy Carter in 1978, directly in response to the Watergate scandal and President Nixon's attempt to treat White House documents as personal property. The law establishes that all presidential records — defined as documents created or received in the course of conducting official presidential duties — belong to the United States government, not the sitting president. Upon leaving office, a president must transfer all records to the National Archives, where they are eventually made available to the public under a 5-to-12-year access restriction. Violating the PRA can constitute a federal crime.
